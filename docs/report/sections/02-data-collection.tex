\section{Data Collection}\label{sec:data}

\subsection{Method}

Collection is split across the three team members, each responsible for a share of the
final reviewed set. The procedure recorded in \texttt{data/COLLECTION\_TEMPLATE.md} is:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em, itemsep=0.2em]
  \item Listen in an ordinary setting -- taxis, chop houses, roadside stalls, bendskin
        ranks, the neighbourhood, the university.
  \item Write down \emph{the exact words spoken}, preserving slang, code-mixing, accents,
        incomplete sentences and mistakes. No cleaning up, no translation.
  \item Have a second Francanglais-familiar member check the transcription against what
        was heard, and record any disagreement rather than resolving it silently.
  \item Explain the coursework and obtain permission before retaining identifiable
        conversation; use anonymous identifiers and avoid unsafe collection settings.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Provenance enforced in software}

Authenticity is not left to good intentions -- it is a validated invariant. Every record
carries a \texttt{source\_kind} of either \texttt{field} or \texttt{demo}, and
\texttt{validate\_import} in \texttt{core/corpus.py} \emph{rejects} any \texttt{field}
record that does not also carry \texttt{manual\_transcription\_attested = true}. A
statement therefore cannot enter the graded corpus without an explicit human attestation
that it was listened to and transcribed by hand.

Statement revisions are append-only: editing a statement creates a new revision rather
than overwriting history, so the wording originally heard remains recoverable. Since the
analyzer assigns statement identifiers and collector identity server-side from the
signed-in account, attribution cannot be mistyped or reassigned by a later editor.

\begin{keypoint}[Why the two source kinds are kept apart]
\texttt{demo} records exist so the pipeline could be developed and tested before
collection finished. They are deliberately marked \texttt{manual\_transcription\_attested
= false} and are excluded from any claim about real usage.
\texttt{AnalysisSnapshot.is\_field\_only()} lets an export verify that a submitted
snapshot contains no synthetic examples.
\end{keypoint}

\subsection{Collected statements}

\begin{todobox}[Field corpus not yet collected]
At the time this report was generated the corpus contains \CorpusTotal\ statements, and
\textbf{all of them are \texttt{demo} fixtures} written to exercise the pipeline. None
carry \texttt{manual\_transcription\_attested = true}, so none of them satisfy
Component~1 of the marking scheme.

\medskip
\textbf{Required before submission.} Collect 10--15 real statements, enter them through
the Corpus view with \texttt{source\_kind = field} and the attestation box ticked, then
re-run \texttt{python tools/export\_tables.py}. The table in
Section~\ref{sec:results} and the lexicon appendix will repopulate automatically from the
real data; no prose in this report needs rewriting.
\end{todobox}

The table of raw collected statements appears in Section~\ref{sec:results}, alongside the
verdict the grammar returns for each, so the collected data and the result it produces can
be read together rather than in separate appendices.

\subsection{Topic coverage}

The assignment lists ten themes. The analyzer models \TopicCount\ topics, matched by an
evidence-cited keyword table (\texttt{TOPIC\_KEYWORDS} in \texttt{core/analysis.py}). A
topic is always reported together with the exact canonical words that triggered it, so a
reviewer can see precisely why a label was attached; topic matching never influences
whether a statement is grammatically accepted.
